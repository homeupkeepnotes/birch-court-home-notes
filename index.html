After the Hail: Checking a Garage Door in Firestone Before You Decide on Repairs

November 24, 2026

Firestone sits on the open plains of southwest Weld County, just east of Longmont along the I-25 corridor, and it shares the Front Range's reputation for strong summer thunderstorms. When a storm drops hail on the Carbon Valley, the garage door is usually the largest flat surface on the front of the house, and it takes the hits head on. Many homes in Firestone's newer neighborhoods have steel doors facing the street, often a double door plus a single on a three-car garage, so there is a lot of door to inspect after a storm.

Here is a calm way to look at a door after hail, separate cosmetic damage from functional damage, and get ready for a repair conversation.

Start with safety, then function

Before you worry about dents, make sure the door still works and is safe to operate.

  • Stand clear and run the door through one full open and close cycle. Listen for grinding, scraping, or popping that was not there before.
  • Watch both sides as it moves. The door should travel evenly, without one side lagging.
  • Check that it reverses when it meets an obstruction. Lay a roll of paper towels on the floor in the door's path and close it; the door should stop and go back up.
  • If the door will not move, moves unevenly, or a cable looks slack or frayed, stop using it and leave it closed if you can.

Hail itself rarely breaks springs, but storms often come with strong wind, and wind can push an open door or a car door into the panels, or flex a door that is closed. Anything that affects the tracks, rollers, cables, or springs is a functional problem and should be fixed before cosmetic work.

Reading the dents

Once you know the door works, look closely at the panels, ideally in low-angle morning or evening light, which makes dents easier to see.

  • Small, shallow dimples spread across the face of the door are common on thin steel doors. They are mostly cosmetic, although they can break the paint and, over time, let rust start.
  • Larger dents along the edge of a section, near the hinges or where sections meet, can matter more. If the edge is bent, the section may not seal properly against its neighbor, and the hinge may bind.
  • Creased or folded areas can weaken a section. A section that is bowed inward can strain the opener and the rollers as it moves through the curve of the track.
  • Damaged windows or trim in decorative doors are worth noting separately.

Take clear photos from a few angles and at least one wide shot showing the whole door and the house. Note the date of the storm. That record is useful no matter how you decide to proceed.

One section or a whole door?

Garage doors are built in horizontal sections, and sometimes it is possible to replace only the damaged ones. That depends on whether matching sections are still available for your door's make, model, color, and texture. Builders in fast-growing towns like Firestone often installed the same door models across a subdivision, which can make matching easier for a door of a certain age and harder for an older or discontinued line. If most of the sections are damaged, or the replacement sections would not match, a full new door may be the more sensible fix.

If you are filing an insurance claim, your insurer will have its own process. The useful thing you can do is provide good photos, describe any functional problems clearly, and get a written estimate that lists what is being replaced and why.

Things to avoid

  • Do not try to pop dents out with heat, suction cups, or a mallet from the inside. Steel doors are thin, and the result is often worse than the original dent.
  • Do not adjust springs or cables to compensate for a door that now moves strangely. Those parts are under high tension.
  • Do not keep running a door that is scraping or binding. Small problems in the track become bigger with every cycle.
